FAQs

Why "standardized," not "standard"?

When a counterparty says "this is our standard contract," that usually means one side wrote it, alone, and is hoping you sign without redlining it, and it often turns out to carry one-sided terms that don't hold up once you actually read them.

A standardized contract is a different animal: the language was negotiated once, in the open, by a working group that includes both sides of the table (or a regulator, or a whole trade), so the terms reflect a real market position rather than one party's opening bid. That's the bar for inclusion here. Three shapes of it show up on this page:

Cover-page templates: a fixed, openly-licensed body of terms (Bonterms, Common Paper, oneNDA) that parties adopt wholesale and customize only through a short front page, rather than redlining the legal text.

Regulator-issued instruments: clauses a government body drafted and published for a specific compliance purpose (the EU SCCs, the UK IDTA), which parties incorporate largely as-is.

Industry-association forms: documents a trade body maintains as the shared default for its sector (ISDA, FIDIC, AIA, ACORD), often gated behind membership or a purchase, but still functioning as the position everyone negotiates from.

How are they created?

By whichever of the three groups above is doing the drafting, but always by more than one side of the table, which is the whole point:

Cover-page templates come out of standing committees of practicing attorneys from different firms and companies (Common Paper's model), or get vetted by a large pool of outside counsel before release (Bonterms cites 120+ lawyers), or get crowdsourced clause by clause from contributing law firms and in-house teams (oneNDA). They're published under an open license and revised in numbered versions as market practice shifts.

Regulator-issued instruments go through a formal rulemaking process: a government body drafts the text, opens it for public comment, and issues a final version with legal effect. That's how the European Commission's SCCs and the UK ICO's IDTA came about.

Industry-association forms come from a trade body convening a working group of member companies and outside counsel to draft and negotiate a base document, then publishing updated editions every several years: the process behind the ISDA Master Agreement, FIDIC's Rainbow Suite, and the AIA contract families.

How do they help?

Faster signature. Both sides spend their time on deal-specific variables instead of re-litigating boilerplate; Common Paper reports 63% of its contracts get signed within 24 hours.

Lower legal spend. A first draft built on a market-tested baseline needs far less outside-counsel review than a bespoke document drafted from scratch.

Fewer one-sided surprises. Because the terms were negotiated by many stakeholders rather than one company's own lawyers, they tend to land closer to where a full negotiation would have ended up anyway.

Actual comparability. When every vendor's DPA or NDA starts from the same template, you can compare positions across a whole portfolio instead of re-reading each one from zero.

Can you still customize them?

Yes, but where that customization happens depends on the type, and that's by design, not a workaround:

Cover-page templates keep the underlying legal language fixed and route every deal-specific choice (parties, dates, dollar caps, opt-in/opt-out elections) onto a short cover page. That routing is the mechanism, not a limitation of it.

Regulator-issued instruments like the SCCs and IDTA ship with built-in modules or annexes you select based on your transfer scenario, plus room for local-law addenda, but the core legal text is fixed by the regulator that issued it.

Industry-association forms get customized through a companion document that sits alongside the base text (an ISDA "Schedule," a FIDIC "Particular Conditions," an AIA amendment) which overrides specific provisions while leaving the rest of the standard document untouched.

The short version: you're negotiating the variables and elections, not the legal prose underneath. That's exactly why it's faster.
